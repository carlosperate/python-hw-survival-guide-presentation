Multimeter: is there power?

  • Shows volts, ohms and amps, and a beep when two points connect.
  • Use it for: checking power, a wire that isn't connected, a short.

Aneng AN8008 or similar: auto-ranging, with a continuity beep. For low-voltage electronics, not mains.

Mark Hennessy: budget multimeters, reviewed

Multimeter: three checks and a trap

  1. Voltage, power on: black on GND, red on 3V3. Expect 3.3 V.
  2. Continuity, power off: a beep means connected. 3V3 to GND must not beep.
  3. Current: the meter goes in the circuit, red lead in the mA jack.

Measure a voltage with the red lead still in the mA jack, and the meter shorts the supply and blows its internal fuse. Current reads 0.00 from then on.
Move it back to V.

USB power meter: how much current?

  • Shows the volts and amps going into your board over USB, as it runs.
  • Use it for: resets when a motor starts, flat batteries, your power budget and calculate battery life.

RD TC66C or any meter that reads to 0.1 mA. It plugs in between the computer and the cable.

What a USB meter misses

  • Short spikes: it reads a few times a second, so a spike can fall between readings.
  • Sleep current: microamps, too small for most USB meters. That's a power profiler's job, such as Nordic's PPK2.
  • On USB, CircuitPython only pretends to sleep. Measure deep sleep on a battery.

When you need a power profiler

  • Shows current as a graph, 100,000 readings a second: microamps (µA) asleep, milliamps (mA) when running.
  • Provides power can also be used as a power source for testing.
  • Use it for: battery life. Toggle a pin from Python to mark your code on the graph.
  • Nordic's Power Profiler Kit II (PPK2), I generally recommend this product, only downside is price ~€100.

Debug probe: a second way in

  • Gives you a serial console on the board's UART pins, and SWD: the chip's own debug port.
  • Use it for: flashing or rescuing a board without its boot button, and logs when USB is busy.

Raspberry Pi Debug Probe: cables and a case included. For the Pico and other Arm boards at 3.3 V.

The probe and your Python

  • A console: its U port on GP0 and GP1, TX to RX, GND first. MicroPython can copy its REPL there.
  • Flashing: SWD writes new firmware with no button to press.
  • Debugging means the C firmware, not your .py. Python debuggers are only now arriving.

You may already have a probe

  • A spare Pico: flash Raspberry Pi's debugprobe firmware and it works as a Debug Probe, for about €4.
  • ESP32-S3, C3 or C6: USB-JTAG is built into the chip. One USB cable, then OpenOCD.
  • BBC micro:bit: its second chip is a probe and a serial port, called DAPLink.

USB-UART adapters: about €5

  • Set it to 3.3 V: 5 V can damage a 3.3 V board.
  • Cross the wires: TX to RX, RX to TX, and GND to GND.
  • Often built in: most ESP32 boards and the micro:bit have one. Some, like the ESP32-CAM, don't.

Logic analyser: did the sensor answer?

  • Shows the 0s and 1s on 8 wires, decoded into I²C, UART or SPI bytes.
  • Use it for: did the sensor answer? What did your code send?

A 24 MHz 8-channel USB analyser and the open source PulseView. Or a spare Pico.

What you'll see

A good read: chip ID 60

Wrong address: no answer

OSError: [Errno 5] EIO · Python says that. The wire says why.

D0, D1: channels · S start · Sr restart · A ACK · N NACK · P stop · hex

Logic analyser: getting started

  • GND first, then a channel per signal: SDA, SCL.
  • Sample at least 4× faster than the signal: 24 MHz is plenty for I²C and UART.
  • Add a decoder for I²C, UART or SPI. PulseView's are written in Python.
  • A bare Pico is 3.3 V only; the €10 analyser copes with 5 V too.

When you need an oscilloscope

  • An analyser sees 0s and 1s. A scope sees the shape of the voltage: a sagging supply, slow I²C edges, PWM.
  • Not a first buy. Later: a FNIRSI 2C53T handheld, about €85, or a Rigol DHO802 bench scope, about €390.
