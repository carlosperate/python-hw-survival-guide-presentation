Drivers

Every light, motor and sensor needs one.

Your Python controlling the real world

Every part needs its own signals, protocol, and timing.

A driver abstracts all of that for you.

The fun part: your code leaves the screen. Lights, motion, a screen, sensing the room. Each part talks differently: a servo wants a pulse width (PWM), a NeoPixel a precise bit timing on one pin, a stepper its coils in sequence, the distance sensor a trigger pulse and an echo to time, the PIR just a pin going high, the BME280, OLED, accelerometer and encoder board registers over I2C, the e-ink screen SPI, and the camera eight data pins at once. You don't learn each language yourself: a driver does. Photos and licences: public/images/parts/CREDITS.md (CC BY-SA, so the cut-outs are too). Next: so first, look for one.

First step, find an existing driver

  1. Read the official docs. Popular vendors will provide all you need.
  2. Find the chip name. Board name might not be the same as the chip (e.g. BME280).
  3. Start with Google, but also use GitHub's search with filters.
  4. Check micropython-lib, awesome-micropython, awesome-circuitpython, popular vendor's repos (Adafruit, SparkFun, Pimoroni, Arduino).
  5. Check it's for your Python. E.g. machine.I2C is MicroPython; busio is CircuitPython.
Most of the time, someone has written it. Search the part number, not the product name: the product page names the chip ("The BME280 is the next-generation of sensors from Bosch", adafruit.com/product/2652). Order: what the firmware already has (help('modules'); stock ESP32 freezes neopixel, apa106, onewire, ds18x20, dht), then micropython-lib (17 drivers on 2026-10-25, no BME280), then awesome-micropython (seven BME280 entries), then the maker. Adafruit's bundle is CircuitPython: "may or may not work with MicroPython" (circuitpython.org/libraries). SparkFun's Qwiic_Py drivers support both; Pimoroni builds theirs into its firmware. Sources: reports/finding-drivers.md. Next: found one? Get it onto the board.

Which bus?

  • A bus is a few wires between chips, plus rules for sending bytes: the protocol.
  • Microcontrollers have hardware for the three common ones built in: UART, I²C and SPI.
  • You rarely choose: the part's datasheet or product page says which bus it speaks.
  • You still need to know it: it decides which pins you wire, and the bus you hand the driver.

i2c = I2C(0, scl=Pin(5), sda=Pin(4))
bme = bme280.BME280(i2c=i2c)
  • Not every part needs a bus: buttons use a plain pin (GPIO), servos use pulses (PWM).
Many here have never wired a bus. A bus is shared wires plus a protocol; the microcontroller's I2C, SPI and UART blocks (last slide) do the bit-level work. The chip's datasheet picks the bus, and some (the BME280) speak two. The driver hides the protocol but not the wiring: you build the bus object and pass it in, as on the install slide. Pins are the Pico's I2C(0) defaults. Not a bus: GPIO, PWM, and NeoPixel, a timing protocol on one GPIO pin. Next: the three buses side by side.

UART: no clock, so agree in advance

UART sends bytes one bit at a time from TX to the other end's RX. There is no clock wire, so both ends agree on the speed, the baud rate, in advance.

'A' = 0x41 = 0100 0001 · sent least significant bit (LSB) first

Idle: the line sits HIGH while nothing is sent.

START: the line drops for one bit. The receiver starts timing from this edge.

Eight data bits, least significant first: 'A' goes out backwards.

STOP: back to HIGH for at least one bit, ready for the next byte.

The receiver samples the middle of each bit at its baud rate. Send at 9600, read at 19200: garbage, and no exception.

machine.UART · SparkFun: Serial Communication

Vertical slide, skipped live. A click per part: idle, START, data, STOP, then where the receiver samples. Line idles HIGH; START is a 1-to-0 transition; data LSB first; STOP returns to 1. 8N1 is the usual framing. The default baud rate is per port: 115,200 on rp2 and ESP32 (ports/*/machine_uart.c), and the pyboard makes you pass it. 104 µs per bit at 9,600, 8.68 µs at 115,200 (derived). SparkFun Serial; machine.UART docs.

I²C: reading the chip ID, bit by bit

SCL is the clock, SDA the data, both shared. The microcontroller (MCU) reads the BME280's ID:

i2c.readfrom_mem(0x76, 0xD0, 1) → b'\x60'

Vertical slide, skipped live. A click per field. Each field's band and cell share a colour; the MCU row is what the microcontroller sends, the BME280 row what the sensor sends. SDA may change only while SCL is LOW; START and STOP are SDA edges while SCL is HIGH; bytes go MSB first, each followed by a ninth-clock ACK; a NACK from a reading controller signals the end (UM10204 3.1.3-3.1.6, 3.1.10). BME280: 7-bit address 0x76 with SDO to GND, chip-ID register 0xD0 = 0x60 (datasheet 5.4.1, 6.2). readfrom_mem() is writeto(addr, reg, stop=False) then readfrom(..., stop=True) (extmod/machine_i2c.c): hence the repeated START. 36 clocks: ~0.36 ms at 100 kHz. The code says 0x76; the wire says 0xEC and 0xED: the 8-bit address trap in "When scan() lies". Tooling replays this read live, field by field.

SPI: to read, you must write

SPI has a clock, a wire each way and a chip-select (CS) per device. Every tick moves one bit each way.

cs(0); spi.write_readinto(b'\xD0\x00', buf); cs(1) → buf[1] == 0x60

Idle: CS is HIGH, so the sensor ignores the bus.

cs(0): CS goes LOW and selects the BME280.

Byte 1: the microcontroller sends 0xD0, the ID register (bit 7 = read). The reply is ignored.

Byte 2: the microcontroller sends a dummy 0x00. Its 8 clocks shift 0x60 back on CIPO.

cs(1): done. No ACK, so no scan(): reading a known ID register is SPI's scan().

COPI and CIPO were MOSI and MISO in older docs · machine.SPI · BME280 datasheet

Vertical slide, skipped live. A click per step: select, register byte, dummy byte, deselect. Full duplex through paired shift registers, usually MSB first. In MicroPython, CS is your own Pin; defaults are polarity 0, phase 0 (machine.SPI). The BME280 supports modes 00 and 11, uses the register MSB as the read/write bit (1 = read), and runs up to 10 MHz (datasheet 6.3). A missing SPI device reads 0x00 or 0xFF silently. Trap: pulling the BME280's CSB low even once disables I2C until the next power-on (6.1).

A driver is a small class


from micropython import const
_ID = const(0xD0)
_CTRL = const(0xF4)
_TEMP = const(0xFA)

class BME280:
    def __init__(self, i2c, addr=0x76):
        self.i2c, self.addr = i2c, addr
        self._buf = bytearray(3)
        if i2c.readfrom_mem(addr, _ID, 1) != b"\x60":
            raise OSError("BME280 not found")
        i2c.writeto_mem(addr, _CTRL, b"\x27")  # wake

    def raw_temperature(self):
        b = self._buf
        self.i2c.readfrom_mem_into(self.addr, _TEMP, b)
        return b[0] << 12 | b[1] << 4 | b[2] >> 4
  1. Registers as const()
  2. Take the bus, don't make it: I2C, SoftI2C or a fake
  3. Check the ID, wake it up
  4. One buffer, reused
The chip-ID read from the stack slide, wrapped in a class, is a driver: the layer you write when nobody has. Every register and value comes from the datasheet. API: machine.I2C readfrom_mem, writeto_mem, readfrom_mem_into. The preallocated buffer is the documented pattern (speed_python.html). The BME280 powers up in sleep mode: ctrl_meas (0xF4) = 0x27 sets temperature and pressure oversampling x1 and normal mode. Verify against datasheet 5.4.5 and run this on the board before showing it. Turning raw counts into °C needs the factory calibration words (0x88-0xA1) and Bosch's compensation formula: in a real project, use a library from micropython-lib or Adafruit. Look for a driver first. Then scan() and read the ID.

Symptom first: "BME280 not found", or OSError EIO. scan() is the first question to ask the bus. scan() probes 0x08-0x77 with zero-length writes (extmod/machine_i2c.c). rp2 maps every non-timeout error to EIO; esp32 maps ESP_FAIL to ENODEV; SCL held too long gives ETIMEDOUT (110). Verify the Pico errno on your board before saying it. Pull-ups: rise time ≈ 0.8473 × 50 kΩ × 50 pF ≈ 2.1 µs against a 300 ns Fast-mode limit (derived from UM10204 7.1). CircuitPython refuses instead: "No pull up found on SDA or SCL; check your wiring". Collisions: two BME280s at 0x76 show as one entry. Tie one SDO high (0x77), use a TCA9548A multiplexer, or a second bus. Pico pins: SDA on an even GPIO, SCL on an odd one; bus id = bit 1 of the pin number. 5 V: a breakout powered from 5 V can pull SDA and SCL up to 5 V, over the RP2040's IOVDD + 0.5 V absolute maximum (datasheet 5.5); power it from 3V3 or use one with level shifting. Why weak pull-ups fail

The pull-up resistor lifts the line. The weaker it is, the slower the rise: at 400 kHz, 300 ns at most.

Rise time: 4.7 kΩ ~0.2 µs ✓ · 50 kΩ ~2.1 µs ✕ · five 4.7 kΩ in parallel = 940 Ω, under the 967 Ω minimum

The Pico's internal pull-ups are 50–80 kΩ: fine on the desk, flaky on wires · NXP UM10204, the I²C specification

Vertical slide: read on its own, skipped live. Thresholds 0.3 and 0.7 VDD (UM10204 3.1.2). The ~967 Ω minimum at 3.3 V and 3 mA is derived: five breakouts with 4.7 kΩ each go below it.

Before you blame the driver

For every board and power supply. Signals need the common ground.

Check the part's supply range and its logic level.

Long wires add capacitance & might need stronger pull-ups (↓ resistance). Remember boards/modules might already have pulls!

Servos and LED strips brown out the board. Keep GND shared.

Shift I²C with a MOSFET. A BSS138-style shifter works on I²C. A TXB0108 doesn't.

Boot, input-only, LED and button pins: read the pinout.

A card per click. Ground: a logic level is a voltage against GND; Adafruit, "there must be a ground connection between the two" (NeoPixel best practices). Voltage: Pico GPIO is "fixed at 3.3 V"; ESP32 VIH max is VDD + 0.3 V; micro:bit pins VDD + 0.3 V; Pico 2 takes 5 V only while powered, never on its ADC pins. Power: Pico 3V3 pin under 300 mA, micro:bit 90 mA (V1). Pull-ups: rise time 0.8473 x R x C, 400 pF limit; about 1 kOhm floor at 3.3 V (UM10204, TI SLVA689). Level shifting: NXP AN10441; TI says the TXB0108 "should not be used" on I2C. Pins: ESP32 GPIO0, 2, 12 at boot, 34-39 input only. Sources: reports/wiring-tips.md. Next: Debugging, when it stops.

I²C: resistors pull HIGH, chips pull LOW

  • Resistors "pull-ups" the SDA (data) and SCL (clock) signals to 3.3 V.
  • The microcontroller and I2C device pull the lines LOW to communicate.
  • Some breakouts/modules bring their own resistors.
  • Pull too strong (low resistance), under ~1 kΩ at 3.3 V: chips can't pull LOW. Disable pull-ups on some boards.
  • Pull too weak (or long wires): signal takes too long to rise to 3V3. Reduce total resistance, or reduce bus frequency.

Reset the board mid-read and the sensor can keep holding SDA LOW, still waiting to finish its byte.

scan() fails until you unplug it.

Fix: drive GPIO to pulse SCL up to nine times, until SDA goes HIGH, then start I²C again. Or power-cycle the sensor.

Vertical slide, skipped live. Open drain: a chip can only pull a line LOW; the resistor pulls it HIGH. Resistors in parallel: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + ...; n equal ones give R / n, so 4.7k / 3 = 1.57k. Floor: (3.3 - 0.4) V / 3 mA = 967 Ohm (UM10204 7.1, TI SLVA689). SparkFun: "make sure only one board has the pull-up resistors connected"; Qwiic boards use a cut-trace jumper. Ceiling: rise time 0.8473 x R x C must stay under 1000 ns at 100 kHz, 300 ns at 400 kHz; wires and devices add C (400 pF max). Stuck bus: UM10204 3.1.16, "If the data line (SDA) is stuck LOW, the controller should send nine clock pulses... If not, then use the HW reset or cycle power". Mid-read: the target is part-way through sending a byte and still drives SDA when its bit is 0. Sources: reports/wiring-tips.md.
