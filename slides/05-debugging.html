Debugging

Code, wiring, power or the part? From the outside they all look the same.

New sensor ready to go, but..

But nothing happens, not even an error. Could it be the...

  • Code? the wrong pins, the wrong config, the wrong constructor?
  • Driver? the wrong defaults, similar but different chip, incomplete implementation?
  • Wiring? signals swapped, a loose jumper, a bad breadboard or solder joint?
  • Power? no power to the sensor, wrong voltage, grounds aren't joined?
  • Part? is it dead, fake, or a different version?

Where do we start?

A made-up but typical case: a suspect per click, and most of the room has done at least one of these. On a laptop the bug is in your code. Here it can be in five places, sometimes two at once, and there is no debugger by default: you can't pause the board and look inside. "Dead" includes a part cooked by 5 V on a 3.3 V pin. Guessing wastes the evening. Agans: the rules cover "Software and Hardware Problems" (Debugging: The 9 Indispensable Rules, 2006). Sources: reports/debugging-tips.md. Next: a method that works for code and wires.

Stop guessing, start debugging

  1. Look first: smoke, heat, a burnt smell? Unplug. Then check the LEDs and wires.
  2. Check power & cables: measure voltage & ground, check signal directions and pins.
  3. Make it fail: the smallest test that consistently reproduces the issue.
  4. Change one thing at a time: and write down what you changed.
    "Remember kids, the only difference between screwing around and science is writing it down."
  5. Get a fresh view: explain it, or ask online/ai. Include code, wiring & the error.
Cheapest and safest first, after David Agans' nine rules: Quit Thinking and Look, Check the Plug, Make it Fail with Divide and Conquer, Change One Thing at a Time with Keep an Audit Trail, Get a Fresh View. Look first: "Feel for any excessively hot components" and burned parts have "a nasty and characteristic smell" (Circuit Basics, How to troubleshoot and repair); Elektor's "See me, feel me, hear me" (Jan Buiting, 2017) uses the senses "for a first diagnosis only". Unplugging first is common practice, not from those sources. Feel near a part, not on it: a shorted regulator can burn. Measure, don't assume: a wire that looks right can be in the wrong row. Make it fail, small: a test that fails every time tells you when a change fixed it; the smallest one says which half the fault is in. Prevention: build and test in small steps, one part working before the next (UC Davis EEC18 lab tips). It won't catch faults that only appear together, like total current. Asking for help: say what you've tried. The quote is the popular wording; Savage credited ballistics expert Alexander Jason (2012 talk). Image: a MythBusters still, not openly licensed. Check power & cables

  • Cable: Some USB cable only charge. Use a known‑good data cable.
  • Battery banks: Some switch off when the board draws too little current.
  • Voltage: Ensure the right voltage level is used for your device and any components.
  • Ground: Has to be shared by every board and supply. Breadboard rails can be split.
  • Under load: Resets when a motor or Wi-Fi starts? A brownout. A meter misses it.
A check per click, cheapest first; SparkFun's order is the supply voltage, then shorts, then each chip's VCC and GND ("The multimeter is your first defense when troubleshooting a system", How to Use a Multimeter). Cable: "A surprising number of USB cables are designed to only handle power and not data"; use a "known-good USB data cable", then another port (Adafruit, Welcome to CircuitPython). Battery pack: the micro:bit hardware page advises against USB battery packs, some of which switch off at low current (tech.microbit.org/hardware/power-supply). Voltage: a meter on 3V3 and GND at the board, then on the part's own pins. Ground: a logic level is a voltage against GND, so every board and supply needs one in common. Breadboards: "the power rails on either side are not connected", and some split each rail in the middle (SparkFun, How to Use a Breadboard). Not on the slide: with the power off, 3V3 to GND must not beep on continuity; a brief chirp is capacitors charging. Under load: a brownout looks like a code bug. ESP32 prints "Brownout detector was triggered", and "if voltage drops rapidly, only partial console output may appear before reset" (ESP-IDF Fatal Errors), hence the cut-off line. Espressif lists the supply, the cable and a capacitor as fixes, the threshold last. reset_cause() maps ESP_RST_BROWNOUT to PWRON_RESET (ports/esp32/modmachine.c), so the software thinks you plugged it in. CircuitPython on ESP32 and nRF52 enters safe mode: "Power dipped. Make sure you are providing enough power." The RP2040/RP2350 port doesn't detect it: the board just resets. A meter averages, so it misses the dip; a USB meter or a scope catches it (Tooling). The graph is illustrative, with no threshold number: the ESP32 default is unverified. Replace it with a real scope capture if you have one. Press down for wiring and solder joints. Next: let the board tell you.

Wiring: look, wiggle, swap

Fine sometimes, and flaky others? Maybe something moved.

A good solder joint is smooth and concave. Lumpy or grainy may not connect at all. Headers must be soldered, not just pushed in.

Press on wires and parts as it runs. Comes and goes as you move? A connection. Changes with just a hand nearby? A floating input: add Pin.PULL_UP/DOWN.

Another wire, another sensor, another board. A part you know works tells you which half is broken.

Adafruit: common soldering problems

Vertical slide: read on its own, skipped live. Illustrative story. Adafruit Guide to Excellent Soldering: a cold joint looks rough, lumpy or grainy, is unreliable and can crack later; a good one has a smooth, concave fillet; reheat with flux until it flows. Wiggling and swapping are common practice, not from a single source. Floating pins: an input with nothing connected, or a wire to nothing, "will report seemingly random changes in pin state, picking up electrical noise from the environment, or capacitively coupling the state of a nearby pin" (Arduino docs, Digital Pins); the same high impedance is what makes capacitive touch sensing work, so a hand nearby is enough. A button wired without a pull resistor is the usual case. MicroPython: Pin(BUTTON_PIN, Pin.IN, Pin.PULL_UP); not every port has PULL_DOWN. CircuitPython: pin.pull = digitalio.Pull.UP.

Good and bad solder joints

  • If you've done the soldering, check your joints carefully
  • Breadboards: the two long rails on each side are separate, and some are split in the middle. Check with a meter.
  • Jumper wires break inside the plastic. If in doubt, swap it.

No cProfile on the board: ticks_us() measures inside the chip, and a spare pin lets you measure from outside on a logic analyser, where pauses and missed interrupts show up. Peter Hinch's micropython-monitor turns asyncio task activity into pin pulses and has a hog detector. Four kinds of silence

Stopped

Traceback lost, board idle at the REPL

→ terminal, log to a file

Restarted

C crash, watchdog, brownout

→ reset_cause()

Frozen

Endless loop, blocking I/O, SDA held LOW

→ Ctrl-C, watchdog

Half working

A hard interrupt handler raised: that interrupt is now off

→ emergency exception buffer

Vertical slide, skipped live. CircuitPython blinks 2 red for an exception (Adafruit troubleshooting). ESP32 C crashes print "Guru Meditation Error" (ESP-IDF Fatal Errors). "If an ISR raises an exception it will not propagate to the main loop. The interrupt will be disabled" (isr_rules): use micropython.alloc_emergency_exception_buf(100) to see the traceback. Only hard handlers are switched off; soft handlers, the default, print a traceback and keep firing (shared/runtime/mpirq.c; reports/micropython-debugging-code.md). Blocking calls stall the asyncio scheduler (micropython-monitor). Stuck: a reset mid-I2C-transfer restarts the board but not the sensor, which can keep SDA LOW until power is cycled.

What only these Pythons give you, one per click, its code on the right. Breakpoint: main.py and the REPL share one globals dict, so after Ctrl-C or an uncaught exception main.py's top-level variables are still at >>> ("Any global variables that were set in boot.py and main.py will still be set in the global context of the REPL", reset_boot docs; shared/runtime/pyexec.c). Not after sys.exit() on rp2 and esp32, or any soft reset (Ctrl-D, tools that soft-reset first). Ctrl-C can't get in after micropython.kbd_intr(-1), inside some blocking C calls, or under a bare except. One way only: you can't carry on. 4095 is illustrative. Locals: MicroPython keeps them in slots, so locals() in a function gives the module's names (cpydiff, core language). peek(): eval() takes globals and locals dicts (py/builtinevex.c). Take the input() calls out before deploying: they wait for ever with nothing attached. CircuitPython's three-argument eval() not checked. exec(): since v1.21 a module whose import raises is dropped from sys.modules (commit a79a6ab), as in CPython, so nothing is left to look at. Or keep the loop in app.run(), import cleanly, then read app.count. CircuitPython: "the REPL is a fresh vm" (README); get_previous_traceback() keeps the last code.py traceback, Ctrl-C included, over a soft reload. Tested 30 October 2026 on MicroPython 1.29's unix port (Docker image micropython/unix): peek(), import against exec() (the terminal is trimmed from that run), locals lost. Not run on a board. Why did it reset?

machine.reset_cause() says why the board last started. Each port tells apart different causes:

  • Picopower-on or watchdog only; machine.reset() uses the watchdog
  • ESP32a brownout reads as power-on, a crash as HARD_RESET
  • micro:bitno reset_cause() at all
  • CircuitPythonmicrocontroller.cpu.reset_reason; RP2350 adds brownout

Another board? Its port's modmachine.c lists what it can tell apart.

Vertical slide, skipped live. Read in MicroPython v1.29.0's source, not run. rp2 (ports/rp2/modmachine.c): reset_cause() only asks the SDK's watchdog_caused_reboot(), so the RUN button, power-on and a brownout all read PWRON_RESET (issues #10898, #13009). machine.reset() is watchdog_reboot(), and machine.deepsleep() ends in machine.reset(), so both should read WDT_RESET, like a real timeout: check on your board. esp32 (ports/esp32/modmachine.c): ESP_RST_SW (machine.reset()), ESP_RST_PANIC (a C crash) and ESP_RST_EXT read HARD_RESET, though ESP-IDF says the original ESP32 never returns ESP_RST_EXT; a brownout reads PWRON_RESET; the three watchdogs WDT_RESET; a deep-sleep wake DEEPSLEEP_RESET; after any soft reset, SOFT_RESET whatever the cause. micro:bit V2: its machine module has no reset_cause() (micropython-microbit-v2, modmachine.c). Other ports: read their modmachine.c. CircuitPython 10.3.1, raspberrypi port: RP2040 can't tell a brownout from power-on; RP2350 reports BROWNOUT and RESET_PIN; a reboot through the watchdog reads SOFTWARE, a WatchDogTimer timeout WATCHDOG. ESP32 boards with a USB-UART bridge print the ROM's rst:0x... line on every boot: keep a terminal open across the reset.

A defensive main.py


import sys, time, machine
from machine import Pin
safe = Pin(SAFE_PIN, Pin.IN, Pin.PULL_UP)
if safe.value():          # LOW at boot: skip
    try:
        import app
        app.run()
    except KeyboardInterrupt:
        raise             # never swallow Ctrl-C
    except Exception as e:
        sys.print_exception(e)
        log_crash(e)      # append to crash.log
        blink(3)
        time.sleep(5)     # Ctrl-C window
        machine.reset()

Its core, a try/except round the app ending in machine.reset(), is the docs' own pattern (reset_boot docs, v1.25 onwards); the safe pin, crash log, blink and Ctrl-C window are my additions: run it on the stage board with a deliberate exception in app.py first. SAFE_PIN is any free pin with a button to GND. machine.WDT isn't on the nrf port (nRF52, micro:bit). sys.print_exception(exc, file) writes a traceback to any file (sys docs). Start the WDT inside app.run() after the safe-boot check, never in boot.py: it "cannot be stopped". v1.29 added machine.mem_backup(), which survives soft reset, machine.reset() and deep sleep (not power-off): keep a consecutive-crash counter there and stop resetting after N, or a startup bug becomes a reboot loop. Only append to flash on a real crash. CircuitPython's hook is safemode.py. Press down for the helpers. Next: when the board won't talk to you at all.

The helpers


def log_crash(e):
    try:
        with open("crash.log", "a") as f:
            f.write("cause=%d t=%d\n" % (machine.reset_cause(), time.time()))
            sys.print_exception(e, f)
    except OSError:
        pass  # full or read-only filesystem: carry on

def blink(n, led=Pin(LED_PIN, Pin.OUT)):  # your board's LED
    for _ in range(n):
        led.on(); time.sleep(0.2)
        led.off(); time.sleep(0.2)

Illustrative story. MicroPython "Soft bricking (failure to boot)": Ctrl-C works "in many cases"; safe mode is "only supported on some ports"; a factory reset erases the flash (rp2: a full erase; ESP32: esptool erase_flash), then reflash. Reflashing without erasing keeps the filesystem, and the bad file. CircuitPython 7+: press reset within about 1 s of start-up; safe mode runs no user code.
